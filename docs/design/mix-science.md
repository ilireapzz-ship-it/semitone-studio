# Mix science: where things sit, mono and stereo, and mud

This is what Semitone's **Smart pan**, the per-channel **Mono** switch, **Mix check** and **Mono check** are built on.
They give a starting point, never the last word: every change can be undone in one step, and you should trust your ears.

## Where each sound sits

| Sound | Where | Mono or stereo | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kick, 808, sub | Centre | Mono | Most of the energy. Centred and mono, it stays strong on phones, club systems and mono speakers. |
| Bass (synth or guitar) | Centre | Mono | The ear can't place low notes. A stereo low end gets weaker when played in mono. |
| Snare, clap | Centre | — | The backbeat sits with the kick. |
| Lead vocal | Centre | Mono | It's what people follow. |
| Vocal double | About 70% to one side, its partner on the other | Mono each | The pair sounds wide while the lead stays clear in the middle. |
| Backing vocals | About 50%, left and right | Mono each | They spread around the lead. |
| Ad-libs | About 30% off centre | Mono | They answer the lead without covering it. |
| Hi-hats | About 25% to one side | — | As on a real kit. |
| Shaker, tambourine, small percussion | About 45%, away from the hats | — | They fill the width. |
| One guitar | About 40% to one side, balanced by something on the other | Mono input | A single part off to the side. |
| Two takes of one guitar part | Hard left and hard right | Mono input each | The classic wide rhythm sound. It holds up in mono because the takes differ. |
| Keys, piano | Centre | Stereo | Spread by their own width. |
| Pads, strings | Centre | Stereo, wide | They sit around the edges, out of the centre. |
| Arpeggio | About 25% off centre | — | It moves without crowding the lead. |
| Effects, risers | Centre | Stereo, wide | |

Semitone puts side-panned sounds on whichever side is lighter, so the mix stays balanced. A double always goes opposite its partner.

## Doubles: record again, don't copy

- A second take of the same part thickens it and makes a chorus lift. Two real takes differ slightly, which is what makes them sound wide.
- A copy of one take, nudged in time and panned (the "Haas" trick), comb-filters. It sounds hollow and can cancel when played in mono.
- Doubles panned hard left and right lose some level in mono. Centred doubles hold up better. Mono check lets you hear the difference.

So Semitone suggests **Record a double**: a second track, panned for you and armed, so you sing or play the part again.

## Mud and masking

- Mud lives in the low-mids, about 200–500 Hz (worst around 250–350 Hz), where nearly every instrument has warmth. When too many parts stack there, the mix sounds thick and unclear.
- Masking is when two sounds share a frequency range at similar levels, so the quieter one disappears.
- The most common fix is a high-pass filter on everything that isn't bass or kick. Sources disagree on where (about 80–120 Hz for most parts, higher for small percussion), so Semitone suggests a value per instrument and you can sweep it by ear.
- A small dip (about 3 dB around 300 Hz) on the less important parts that share the low-mids leaves room for the rest. Cut what doesn't own the band; leave what does.

## Low end in mono

- Low end below about 100–150 Hz is usually kept mono. Club systems often sum it to mono anyway, and stereo bass caused problems on vinyl.
- Some producers find forcing everything below 150 Hz to mono makes the low end thin. Semitone doesn't force it on the whole mix: it makes the bass and kick channels mono, and Mono check lets you listen.
